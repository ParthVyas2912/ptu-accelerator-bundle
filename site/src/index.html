What these 22 things
actually are.

If you have never used any of them, start here. One minute now makes the rest of this site read very differently.

What they are

  • Published starting points: working code, templates and instructions, most of them open source and free to take.
  • Software your team deploys into your Azure environment, then adapts to your data, your rules and your users.
  • A way to skip the first eighty per cent of building something. The last twenty per cent is still yours.

What they are not

  • Not products you buy, and not licensed or supported Microsoft services.
  • Not production-ready, and not certified against any security, privacy or regulatory regime.
  • Not quietly graded: each solution states what was actually tested here and what was not.

Not everything here is an app

Each card carries a tag for what would actually arrive. It is the fastest way to rule something out.

  1. Deployable app

    Software with a screen. Deploy it and people can use it.

  2. Codebase or sample

    Code for developers to build on. There is no finished product in the box.

  3. Configuration or service

    A setting, or a Microsoft service your application talks to. Nothing to install.

  4. Design or workshop

    A plan on paper, or training for your engineers. No software arrives.

How to tell what is actually proven

  • Read the evaluation notesEvery solution ends with what this catalog established, what it could not, and what remains untested. It is written in plain prose rather than graded with a badge, because a badge invites skimming.
  • Tested here is narrower than it soundsWhere tests passed, they passed on chosen scenarios with limited material. That is a reason to look closer, not a guarantee for your data or your questions.
  • Some of these did not workSeveral entries record a real failure, and a few have no confirmed package at all. They stay in the catalog, described honestly, instead of quietly disappearing.
  • Nothing here was reviewed for your environmentPermissions, privacy, safety, monitoring and support are decided in your tenant, by your people, before anything reaches real users.

Nothing here is a recommendation to buy. Every solution names an honest next step, and for several of them that step is "do not build on this yet".

Choose capacity after you understand the workload

Provisioned Throughput Units (PTUs) reserve model-processing capacity; they are not a requirement for using this catalog. Verify model, API & geography compatibility. Standard and Batch remain valid alternatives.

Three checks before you scale

Quality first.
Capacity second.

Good answers still depend on the model, trusted source material, task design and review. A faster wrong answer is still wrong.

  • Match the deployment. The model, API, deployment type and geography must support the intended route. Capacity is not interchangeable everywhere.
  • Count the whole service. Search, storage, hosting, document processing and speech can cost extra.
  • Keep alternatives on the table. Standard is a valid option for variable or low-volume use; Batch can suit work that does not need an immediate answer.
What decision are you making?

Existing capacity

Make useful adoption the goal.

Start with a compatible, reviewed workflow. Use existing capacity where it helps, then measure successful tasks, quality and real utilization.

  1. Check the model, API and geographic fit.
  2. Pilot with real users and approved material.
  3. Base renewal on useful demand—not activity created just to fill capacity.

Decision rule: existing spend is not a reason to keep a low-value workflow or renew unused capacity.

New capacity

Prove demand before a purchase.

First establish acceptable answer quality. Then measure the workload’s volume, concurrency and response-time needs using representative tasks.

  1. Validate the outcome, not just that a demo runs.
  2. Compare Standard, Batch and compatible provisioned options.
  3. Purchase only when actual demand and total cost justify the commitment.

Decision rule: no fixed PTU quantity or savings promise follows from this portfolio.

Before you decide

Good questions.
Straight answers.

Useful boundaries for a stakeholder conversation.

Print view includes both capacity options and the full 22-candidate summary, regardless of active filters.

Can we use this in production today?

Not on the strength of these results alone. The portfolio supports guided demonstrations only. Each chosen workflow needs representative evaluation, permissions, data handling, safety review, monitoring and an agreed operational support arrangement before production use.

Does buying PTUs make answers more accurate?

No. PTUs reserve model-processing capacity. Accuracy and usefulness depend on the model, source material, retrieval, task design and evaluation. Capacity cannot repair a failed grounded answer or a defective workflow.

Are all 22 candidates PTU-ready applications?

No. The inventory includes engines, foundations, proposed workflows, specialist options and unsuccessful prototypes. The three bundles are outcome groupings, not 22 ready apps. PTU fit is conditional on supported models, APIs, deployment types, geography and measured demand.

Is Voice Live excluded from using PTUs?

No. Official Voice Live documentation describes a Bring Your Own Model path that can use PTU deployments. That integration is untested here; only managed text probes were verified. Speech processing has separate charges, and text probes do not validate the complete voice experience.

What if we already have capacity—or very little demand?

For existing PTUs, focus on useful adoption and evidence for renewal. Do not create work just to consume capacity. For new purchases, establish quality and actual demand first. Standard and Batch are legitimate alternatives; the right choice may be not to purchase or renew PTUs.

Are the five new workflows already deployed?

No. They are planned implementations. Controlled drafts are the highest priority and must not be confused with the marketing-oriented Content Generation repository, which was only compiled. RFP and contract review is also a proposed procurement priority, not an implemented capability. Voice Live BYOM is a separate integration test, not a sixth new workflow.

What does public-sector applicability mean?

The workflows address common needs in DND and other public-sector settings: document work, evidence, requirements and controlled decisions. This is an independent working portfolio—not an official product, endorsement, compliance certification or support commitment. Suitability and authorization must be assessed for the intended environment and information.

Who can access the GitHub repository?

The repository is private. Only contributors with explicitly granted access can view it; a GitHub sign-in alone does not grant access. The public website is intentionally separate from the private implementation and evaluation material.
